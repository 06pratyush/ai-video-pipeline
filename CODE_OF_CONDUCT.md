# Code of Conduct

## The short version

Be kind, be specific, be patient.

Bug reports and feature ideas are welcome. Bad-faith arguments and personal attacks are not.

## Why this exists

AI Video Studio is maintained by a very small team in their own time. The practical consequence is
that goodwill is the project's scarcest resource — more than compute, more than review capacity. A
thread that turns hostile costs far more than the bug it was about, because it makes the next person
hesitate before opening an issue at all.

So this is not boilerplate. It is the operating condition that keeps the project open.

## What is expected

**In issues and pull requests**

- Describe problems concretely. "It doesn't work" costs a maintainer three questions before anything
  can start; an OS, GPU model, VRAM figure and the exact steps you took often resolves it in one
  reply.
- Assume the other person has a reason. Code that looks wrong is often code working around something
  you have not hit yet. Ask before concluding.
- Accept that "no" is a legitimate answer, and that it is usually about maintenance burden rather
  than about the quality of your idea. [REVIEW_AND_MERGE.md](REVIEW_AND_MERGE.md) sets out the
  reasons a change gets declined and what would change the answer.
- Remember you are talking to a volunteer. Urgency on your side does not create obligation on
  theirs.

**When reviewing**

- Review the diff, not the author. A first-time contributor's patch gets the same reading as a
  maintainer's.
- Say what would make it acceptable. A rejection without a path forward is not a review.
- Label your comments by weight — **blocking:**, **should-fix:**, **nit:** — so nobody has to guess
  which of your opinions are mandatory.
- Praise what was done well, when it was. It costs one line and it is why people come back.

**Generally**

- Give people the benefit of the doubt across a language barrier. Terseness is not rudeness, and
  imperfect English is not imprecision of thought.
- Keep disagreement about the work. "This approach has a race condition" is useful; "you clearly
  don't understand threading" is not, even when the first sentence is true.

## What is not acceptable

- Personal attacks, insults, or derogatory comments
- Harassment of any kind, public or private, including unwanted contact after being asked to stop
- Discrimination or demeaning remarks relating to gender, gender identity, sexual orientation,
  disability, physical appearance, body size, race, ethnicity, nationality, age, caste or religion
- Sexual language or imagery, and unwelcome sexual attention
- Publishing someone's private information without their explicit permission
- Deliberate intimidation, sustained disruption of discussions, or brigading a thread
- Arguing in bad faith: moving goalposts, misrepresenting what someone said, or re-litigating a
  settled decision to exhaust the other side
- Using the issue tracker as a platform for unrelated grievances
- Submitting code you do not have the right to contribute, or passing off others' work as your own

## Scope

This applies in every project space — issues, pull requests, discussions, commit messages, code
comments, and release threads — and to conduct outside those spaces when it is directed at project
participants because of their involvement here.

## Reporting a problem

Report conduct issues privately to the repository owner,
[@06pratyush](https://github.com/06pratyush), through GitHub's direct messaging. Do not open a public
issue about someone's behaviour; that escalates a situation this process exists to de-escalate.

A useful report includes links to the relevant comments, roughly what happened, and what you would
like to see change. You do not need to prove intent, and you do not need to have handled it
perfectly yourself before reporting it.

**What happens next:** acknowledgement within 72 hours, and the report is treated as confidential.
Your identity is not shared with the person reported without your agreement. If a report concerns
the owner, you are under no obligation to raise it here — GitHub's own
[abuse reporting](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/maintaining-your-safety-on-github/reporting-abuse-or-spam)
goes to a party independent of this project, and that is the right route.

Reports made in bad faith — to retaliate, or to remove someone from a disagreement — are themselves
a violation.

## Enforcement

The response is proportionate to what happened, and the reasoning is explained to the person
affected:

| Response | When |
|---|---|
| **A private word** | A first lapse, probably a bad day or a misread tone |
| **A public correction** | Conduct in a thread that needs a visible line drawn, so bystanders know the norm |
| **A temporary block** | Repeated conduct after being asked to stop, or a single serious incident |
| **A permanent ban** | Harassment, threats, or a pattern that continues after a temporary block |

Locking or closing a thread is a cooling-off measure, not a verdict, and it is not a punishment of
whoever commented last.

The repository owner makes these decisions. Where a decision was wrong it will be said so and
reversed; being the owner is not an argument.
